7 Mean and Varaince Assumptions

7.1 Introduction

Up to this point, linear regression has been studied primarily as an optimization problem. Given an observed response vector \(\mathbf{y}\) and an observed design matrix \(\mathbf{X}\), least squares chooses the coefficient vector that minimizes the residual sum of squares.

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \underset{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{\operatorname{argmin}} \; (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})' (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}). \]

When \(\mathbf{X}\) has full column rank, the solution is

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}. \]

No probabilistic assumptions are required to obtain this result. The quantities \(\mathbf{X}\) and \(\mathbf{y}\) can be treated as fixed observed values, and least squares determines which coefficient vector gives the best fit according to the squared-error criterion.

In this chapter, we change perspective. Instead of asking only which coefficients best fit the data that were observed, we propose a statistical model for how the responses are generated. The coefficients will now be interpreted as unknown parameters of this data-generating mechanism, while some components of the response will be treated as random.

Thus, the focus changes from

\[ \text{fitting an observed response} \]

to

\[ \text{modeling a random response generated by an underlying mechanism}. \]

The least-squares estimator will remain the same computational quantity. However, because the response is now random, the estimator \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) and other quantities obtained from \(\mathbf{y}\) will also be random. This will allow us to study their expectations, variances, and other statistical properties.

7.1.1 A More Complete Data-Generating Mechanism

The explanatory variables included in a regression model are rarely the only quantities that affect the response. Some relevant explanatory variables may be observed, while others may be unavailable or unmeasured. In addition, the recorded response may contain measurement error or other sources of variation.

Suppose that the response is affected by three types of contributions:

  1. Observed explanatory variables, which are available to us and included in the regression model.
  2. Unobserved explanatory variables, which affect the response but are not available to us.
  3. Additional discrepancies, such as measurement error or other sources of variation that are not explicitly represented by the explanatory variables.

Let \(\mathbf{X}\) denote the design matrix containing the observed explanatory variables and let \(\mathbf{Z}\) denote a matrix containing the unobserved explanatory variables.

A more complete data-generating mechanism can then be written as

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \mathbf{u}. \]

In this representation, \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) contains the unknown parameters associated with the observed explanatory variables, while \(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\) contains the unknown parameters associated with the unobserved explanatory variables.

The vector \(\mathbf{u}\) represents additional discrepancies that are not captured by either group of explanatory variables.

The three components therefore play different roles:

\[ \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} = \text{contribution from observed explanatory variables}, \]

\[ \mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\gamma} = \text{contribution from unobserved explanatory variables}, \]

and

\[ \mathbf{u} = \text{additional unobserved discrepancies}. \]

The first component can be represented explicitly because \(\mathbf{X}\) is observed. The remaining components cannot generally be modeled separately because \(\mathbf{Z}\) and \(\mathbf{u}\) are not observed.

This motivates a reduced representation of the data-generating mechanism.

7.1.2 The Reduced Regression Model

Because the contributions from the unobserved explanatory variables and the additional discrepancies cannot generally be separated using the observed data, we combine them into a single error term.

Definition 7.1 (Regression Error) The regression error vector is defined as

\[ \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \mathbf{u}. \]

The error vector represents the combined contribution to the response of the components that are not explicitly represented by the observed explanatory variables in \(\mathbf{X}\).

Using this definition, the more complete mechanism

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \mathbf{u} \]

can be written as

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{e}. \]

Thus, we obtain the familiar linear regression model

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{e}. } \]

This equation has exactly the same algebraic form used in the previous chapters, but its interpretation is now different.

Previously, \(\mathbf{e}\) could be viewed simply as the discrepancy between the observed response and the linear component of the model. We now interpret \(\mathbf{e}\) as a random component of the data-generating mechanism.

Similarly, \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is no longer simply a coefficient vector chosen to fit the observed response. It is an unknown parameter vector describing the contribution of the observed explanatory variables to the response.

The reduced model therefore decomposes the response into two components:

\[ \mathbf{y} = \underbrace{ \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} }_{\text{component represented by observed explanatory variables}} + \underbrace{ \mathbf{e} }_{\text{unobserved component}}. \]

At this point, this decomposition does not impose any assumptions on the mean or variance of \(\mathbf{e}\).

In particular, it is possible that

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] \neq \mathbf{0}. \]

Treating \(\mathbf{X}\) as fixed, the mean of the response would then satisfy

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}]. \]

Therefore, if

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] \neq \mathbf{0}, \]

then

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] \neq \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}. \]

In this case, the unobserved component contains a systematic contribution to the mean of the response that is not represented by \(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\).

This leads naturally to the first probabilistic assumption of the regression model. We will impose a condition on the mean of \(\mathbf{e}\) that allows \(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) to represent the mean of the response.

This is the mean assumption.

7.2 Mean Assumptions

We begin by imposing an assumption on the mean of the regression errors. For each observation, assume that

\[ \mathbb{E}[e_i] = 0, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n. \]

Equivalently, in vector form,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] = \mathbf{0}. } \]

The introduction motivated this assumption by asking when the component \(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) can be interpreted as the mean of the response. The following result answers that question directly.

Proposition 7.1 (Mean of the Response) Consider the regression model

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{e}, \]

where \(\mathbf{X}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) are treated as fixed. Then

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] = \mathbf{0} } \]

if and only if

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}. } \]

Proof

Proof. Using the regression model,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] = \mathbf{0} &\iff \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} && \text{alg.} \\ &\iff \mathbb{E} \left[ \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{e} \right] = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} && \text{lin. exp.} \\ &\iff \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} && \text{reg. model}. \end{aligned} \]

The proposition gives a precise interpretation of the mean assumption. It establishes the equivalence

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] = \mathbf{0} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}. } \]

Therefore, the mean assumption is not merely a sufficient condition for interpreting \(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) as the mean response. It is exactly the condition that makes this interpretation valid within the regression model.

Under the mean assumption, the regression decomposition

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{e} \]

can therefore be interpreted as

\[ \text{response} = \text{mean response} + \text{random deviation from the mean}. \]

Conversely, if we want \(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) to represent the mean response, then the error term must satisfy

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] = \mathbf{0}. \]

If instead

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] \neq \mathbf{0}, \]

then

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] \neq \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}, \]

and the unobserved component contains a systematic contribution to the mean of the response that is not represented by the linear component \(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\).

Thus, the mean assumption can be understood equivalently as either a condition on the errors,

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] = \mathbf{0}, \]

or as a statement about the mean structure of the response,

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}. \]

With this equivalence established, we can study what this mean structure implies for the quantities obtained from the least-squares fit.

Proposition 7.2 (Mean Properties of the Least-Squares Quantities) Suppose that \(\mathbf{X}\) has full column rank and that

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] = \mathbf{0}. \]

Then the least-squares estimator satisfies

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] = \boldsymbol{\beta}, } \]

the fitted values satisfy

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}], } \]

and the residuals satisfy

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] = \mathbf{0}. } \]

Proof

Proof. Recall that

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}. \]

Then,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] &= \mathbb{E} \left[ (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y} \right] \\ &= (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}' \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] && \text{lin. exp.} \\ &= (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}' \mathbf{X} \boldsymbol{\beta} && \text{Prop. Mean of Response} \\ &= \boldsymbol{\beta}. \end{aligned} \]

Next, since

\[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{X} \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}, \]

we obtain

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \mathbf{X} \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \\ &= \mathbf{X} \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] && \text{lin. exp.} \\ &= \mathbf{X} \boldsymbol{\beta} && \text{unbiasedness} \\ &= \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] && \text{Prop. Mean of Response}. \end{aligned} \]

Finally, since

\[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} = \mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}, \]

we have

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] - \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] && \text{lin. exp.} \\ &= \mathbf{0} && \text{same mean}. \end{aligned} \]

The first result in the proposition establishes that

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] = \boldsymbol{\beta}. } \]

Therefore, the least-squares estimator is an unbiased estimator of the coefficient vector \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\).

This result provides an important connection with the previous chapters. The quantity

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y} \]

was originally obtained as the solution to an optimization problem. No new estimator has been introduced here. Instead, the same mathematical object is now treated as a random estimator, and the mean assumption gives it a statistical property:

\[ \text{least-squares solution} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{unbiased estimator of } \boldsymbol{\beta}. \]

The second result shows that

\[ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}]. \]

Thus, the response vector \(\mathbf{y}\) and the fitted-value vector \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) have the same mean.

This does not imply that \(\mathbf{y}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) have the same distribution or the same variability. At this point, no assumptions have been imposed on the variance of the errors, so we cannot yet compare their covariance matrices.

The final result gives

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] = \mathbf{0}. } \]

This result should be distinguished from the deterministic least-squares property that, when an intercept is included,

\[ \mathbf{1}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} = 0. \]

The identity

\[ \mathbf{1}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} = 0 \]

is an algebraic property of the least-squares solution. It holds for each observed dataset when an intercept is included.

In contrast,

\[ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] = \mathbf{0} \]

is a probabilistic result. It describes the behavior of the residual vector across repetitions of the data-generating mechanism and follows from the assumption

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] = \mathbf{0}. \]

The logical structure of the section can therefore be summarized as

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] = \mathbf{0} \]

implies

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}, \]

which in turn implies

\[ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] = \boldsymbol{\beta}, \]

\[ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}], \]

and

\[ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] = \mathbf{0}. \]

All of these conclusions require only the mean assumption. They describe the centers of the random quantities involved in the regression model, but they do not describe how much those quantities vary or how their components depend on one another.

The next question is therefore not about the mean, but about variability:

\[ \text{How variable are } \mathbf{y}, \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}, \hat{\mathbf{y}}, \text{ and } \hat{\mathbf{e}} \text{?} \]

Answering this question requires assumptions about

\[ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{e}]. \]

This motivates the variance assumptions considered next.

7.3 Variance Assumptions

The mean assumption specifies the first moment of the regression errors. We now impose assumptions on their second moments.

In particular, we assume that all errors have the same finite, positive variance and that distinct errors are uncorrelated:

\[ \mathbb{V}[e_i] = \sigma^2, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n, \]

where

\[ 0 < \sigma^2 < \infty, \]

and

\[ \mathbb{C}[e_i,e_j] = 0, \qquad i\neq j. \]

Equivalently, in vector form,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{e}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}. } \]

At this point, we are making assumptions only about the covariance structure of the errors. In particular,

\[ \boxed{ \text{uncorrelated does not imply independent}. } \]

We are also not assuming any particular probability distribution for \(\mathbf{e}\). Independence and distributional assumptions will require additional conditions introduced later.

The variance assumption first determines the covariance structure of the response.

Proposition 7.3 (Variance of the Response) Consider the regression model

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{e}, \]

where \(\mathbf{X}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) are treated as fixed. Then

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{e}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I} } \]

if and only if

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}. } \]

Proof

Proof. Using the regression model,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}] &= \mathbb{V} \left[ \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{e} \right] \\ &= \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{e}] && \text{add. const.} \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{e}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}. \]

The proposition establishes that the variance assumption can be expressed equivalently as an assumption on the errors or as an assumption on the response:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{e}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}. } \]

This equivalence follows because \(\mathbf{y}\) and \(\mathbf{e}\) differ only by the fixed vector \(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\). Adding a fixed vector changes the mean but does not change the covariance matrix.

Thus, the variance assumption can be interpreted equivalently as saying that the errors are uncorrelated with common variance \(\sigma^2\), or that the responses are uncorrelated with common variance \(\sigma^2\) when the design matrix is treated as fixed.

Combining this equivalence with the corresponding result for the mean gives

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] = \mathbf{0} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}, \]

and

\[ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{e}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}. \]

Therefore, under the mean and variance assumptions, the first two moments of the errors and the first two moments of the response provide equivalent descriptions of the same model structure.

Proposition 7.4 (Variance Properties of the Least-Squares Quantities) Suppose that \(\mathbf{X}\) has full column rank and

\[ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{e}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}. \]

Then the least-squares estimator satisfies

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] = \sigma^2 (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}, } \]

the fitted values satisfy

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] = \sigma^2\mathbf{H}, } \]

and the residuals satisfy

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] = \sigma^2 (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}). } \]

Proof

Proof. Recall that

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] &= \mathbb{V} \left[ (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y} \right] \\ &= (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}' \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}] \mathbf{X} (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} && \text{var. trans.} \\ &= (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}' (\sigma^2\mathbf{I}) \mathbf{X} (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} && \text{Prop. Variance of Response} \\ &= \sigma^2 (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X} (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \\ &= \sigma^2 (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}. \end{aligned} \]

Next, recall that

\[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{y}. \]

Then,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] &= \mathbb{V} \left[ \mathbf{H}\mathbf{y} \right] \\ &= \mathbf{H} \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}] \mathbf{H}' && \text{var. trans.} \\ &= \mathbf{H} (\sigma^2\mathbf{I}) \mathbf{H}' && \text{Prop. Variance of Response} \\ &= \sigma^2 \mathbf{H}\mathbf{H} && \text{$\mathbf{H}'=\mathbf{H}$} \\ &= \sigma^2 \mathbf{H} && \text{$\mathbf{H}^2=\mathbf{H}$}. \end{aligned} \]

Finally, recall that

\[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} = (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{y}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] &= \mathbb{V} \left[ (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{y} \right] \\ &= (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}) \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}] (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})' && \text{var. trans.} \\ &= (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}) (\sigma^2\mathbf{I}) (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})' && \text{Prop. Variance of Response} \\ &= \sigma^2 (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}) (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}) && \text{$(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})'=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}$} \\ &= \sigma^2 (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}) && \text{$(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})^2=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}$}. \end{aligned} \]

The first result shows that

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] = \sigma^2 (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}. } \]

This gives a new statistical interpretation to an object already encountered in the deterministic least-squares problem.

Previously,

\[ (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \]

appeared as part of the algebraic expression for the least-squares solution. It now also determines the covariance structure of the estimator.

Thus, the geometry and relationships among the explanatory variables, represented through \((\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\), determine how variable the estimated coefficients are.

The second result gives

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] = \sigma^2\mathbf{H}. } \]

From the Mean Assumptions section,

\[ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}], \]

but their covariance matrices are different:

\[ \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] = \sigma^2\mathbf{H}, \]

while

\[ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}. \]

The difference between these covariance matrices is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}] - \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] &= \sigma^2\mathbf{I} - \sigma^2\mathbf{H} \\ &= \sigma^2 (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}). \end{aligned} \]

Since \(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}\) is positive semidefinite,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}] - \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] \succeq \mathbf{0}. } \]

Therefore, in the positive-semidefinite ordering,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] \preceq \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}]. } \]

This gives a precise sense in which the fitted values have a smaller covariance matrix than the original response.

The diagonal elements give an observation-specific interpretation. Since

\[ \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] = \sigma^2\mathbf{H}, \]

we have

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V}[\hat{y}_i] = \sigma^2h_{ii}. } \]

Because

\[ 0 \leq h_{ii} \leq 1, \]

it follows that

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V}[\hat{y}_i] = \sigma^2h_{ii} \leq \sigma^2 = \mathbb{V}[y_i]. } \]

Consequently,

\[ \boxed{ h_{ii} = \frac{ \mathbb{V}[\hat{y}_i] }{ \mathbb{V}[y_i] }. } \]

This provides another interpretation of leverage. The value \(h_{ii}\) measures the proportion of the variance of \(y_i\) that appears in its fitted value \(\hat{y}_i\).

The final result of the proposition gives

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] = \sigma^2 (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}). } \]

The diagonal elements therefore satisfy

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V}[\hat{e}_i] = \sigma^2 (1-h_{ii}). } \]

Thus,

\[ h_{ii} \uparrow \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbb{V}[\hat{e}_i] \downarrow. \]

A high-leverage observation therefore has a residual with smaller variance. This is one reason why raw residual magnitudes are not directly comparable across observations.

There is also a useful complementarity between the fitted value and residual at each observation:

\[ \mathbb{V}[\hat{y}_i] = \sigma^2h_{ii}, \]

while

\[ \mathbb{V}[\hat{e}_i] = \sigma^2(1-h_{ii}). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V}[\hat{y}_i] + \mathbb{V}[\hat{e}_i] = \sigma^2 = \mathbb{V}[y_i]. } \]

The covariance matrices of the fitted values and residuals also recover the same projection matrices that appeared in the deterministic geometry of least squares:

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] &= \sigma^2\mathbf{H}, \\ \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] &= \sigma^2(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}). \end{aligned} } \]

Recall that \(\mathbf{H}\) projects onto the column space of the design matrix,

\[ \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}), \]

while \(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}\) projects onto its orthogonal complement,

\[ \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})^\perp. \]

Thus, the same complementary projection matrices that determined the geometry of fitted values and residuals now determine their covariance structures.

The logical progression from the Mean Assumptions and Variance Assumptions sections can therefore be summarized as

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] = \mathbf{0} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{means and unbiasedness}, \]

while

\[ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{e}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{variances and covariances}. \]

None of the results in this section require the errors to be Normal or independent. They depend only on the assumptions about the first two moments of the errors.

7.3.1 Cross-Covariances of Least-Squares Quantities

The variance assumption allows us to study not only the covariance matrices of the least-squares quantities individually, but also their cross-covariances.

In particular, two cross-covariances will be important later:

\[ \mathbb{C} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}, \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] \]

and

\[ \mathbb{C} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}, \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right]. \]

Both results follow directly from the projection structure established in the previous chapter.

Proposition 7.5 (Zero Cross-Covariances of Least-Squares Quantities) Suppose that

\[ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{e}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{C} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}, \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] = \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}, } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{C} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}, \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] = \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times(p+1)}. } \]

Proof

Proof. Recall that

\[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} = (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{y} \]

and

\[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{y}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{C} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}, \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] &= \mathbb{C} \left[ (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{H}\mathbf{y} \right] \\ &= (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}) \mathbb{C}[\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}] \mathbf{H}' && \text{cov. trans.} \\ &= (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}) \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}] \mathbf{H}' && \text{def. variance} \\ &= (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}) (\sigma^2\mathbf{I}) \mathbf{H} && \text{$\mathbf{H}'=\mathbf{H}$} \\ &= \sigma^2 (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}) \mathbf{H} \\ &= \sigma^2 (\mathbf{H}-\mathbf{H}^2) \\ &= \sigma^2 (\mathbf{H}-\mathbf{H}) && \text{$\mathbf{H}^2=\mathbf{H}$} \\ &= \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}. \end{aligned} \]

Now recall that

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}. \]

Then,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{C} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}, \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] &= \mathbb{C} \left[ (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{y}, (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y} \right] \\ &= (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}) \mathbb{C}[\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}] \mathbf{X} (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} && \text{cov. trans.} \\ &= (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}) \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}] \mathbf{X} (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} && \text{def. variance} \\ &= (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}) (\sigma^2\mathbf{I}) \mathbf{X} (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \\ &= \sigma^2 (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}) \mathbf{X} (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \\ &= \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times(p+1)} && \text{$(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{0}$}. \end{aligned} \]

The first result establishes that the residual vector and the fitted-value vector are uncorrelated:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{C} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}, \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] = \mathbf{0}. } \]

This result is closely related to, but fundamentally different from, a property established in the deterministic treatment of least squares:

\[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{y}} = 0. \]

The two zero quantities have different meanings and even different dimensions.

The identity

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{y}} = 0 \in \mathbb{R} } \]

is a deterministic geometric statement. For every observed dataset, the residual vector is orthogonal to the fitted-value vector.

In contrast,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{C} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}, \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] = \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n} } \]

is a probabilistic statement. It describes the relationship between the random vectors across repeated realizations of the data-generating mechanism.

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \text{orthogonality is a deterministic geometric property}, } \]

whereas

\[ \boxed{ \text{zero cross-covariance is a probabilistic property}. } \]

The second result establishes that

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{C} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}, \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] = \mathbf{0}. } \]

Therefore, the residual vector and the least-squares estimator are also uncorrelated.

The key algebraic identity in the proof,

\[ (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{0}, \]

is a consequence of the same projection geometry developed in the previous chapter. The columns of \(\mathbf{X}\) belong to

\[ \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}), \]

while \(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}\) projects onto

\[ \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})^\perp. \]

Therefore,

\[ (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{0}. \]

The zero cross-covariance is consequently not an isolated algebraic fact. It follows from the same geometric structure underlying least squares:

\[ \boxed{ \text{linear algebra} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{projection geometry} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{probabilistic consequence}. } \]

At this point, however, zero cross-covariance does not imply independence. In general,

\[ \mathbb{C} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}, \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] = \mathbf{0} \]

does not imply that \(\hat{\mathbf{e}}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) are independent, and

\[ \mathbb{C} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}, \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] = \mathbf{0} \]

does not imply that \(\hat{\mathbf{e}}\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) are independent.

These conclusions require an additional distributional assumption.

In the next chapter, we will introduce the Normality assumption. Under that assumption, the relevant least-squares quantities will be jointly Normal. Zero cross-covariance will then imply independence. In particular,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{C} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}, \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] = \mathbf{0} \quad+\quad \text{joint Normality} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat{\mathbf{e}} \perp \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}. } \]

This result will become especially important because the estimator of \(\sigma^2\) will be constructed from the residual vector \(\hat{\mathbf{e}}\). The resulting independence between \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) and quantities constructed from \(\hat{\mathbf{e}}\) will later be used to derive the Student \(t\) distributions required for inference on the regression coefficients.

Thus, the progression across the deterministic, variance, and Normality results is

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{y}} = 0 \quad\longrightarrow\quad \mathbb{C} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}, \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] = \mathbf{0} \quad \overset{\text{Normality}}{\longrightarrow} \quad \hat{\mathbf{e}} \perp \hat{\mathbf{y}}. } \]

A parallel progression holds for the relationship between the residual vector and the least-squares estimator.

7.4 Gauss–Markov Theorem

The least-squares estimator

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y} \]

was originally obtained by minimizing the residual sum of squares. Its derivation required no probabilistic assumptions.

After introducing the mean and variance assumptions, however, we can ask a different question:

\[ \text{Why should we prefer OLS among other linear unbiased estimators?} \]

The Gauss–Markov theorem provides an answer.

At this point, the assumptions accumulated for the regression model are

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{e}, \]

with \(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(p+1)}\) treated as fixed and having full column rank, together with

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] = \mathbf{0} \]

and

\[ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{e}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}, \qquad 0<\sigma^2<\infty. \]

These assumptions are sufficient for the Gauss–Markov result. In particular, Normality is not required.

Proposition 7.6 (Gauss--Markov) Consider the linear regression model

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{e}, \]

where \(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(p+1)}\) is fixed and has full column rank. Suppose that

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] = \mathbf{0} \]

and

\[ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{e}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}. \]

Let

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y} \]

be the ordinary least-squares estimator.

Then, for every linear unbiased estimator \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) of \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\),

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] - \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \succeq \mathbf{0}. } \]

Therefore, the OLS estimator has the smallest covariance matrix among all linear unbiased estimators, in the positive-semidefinite ordering.

Proof

Proof. Let \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) be any linear unbiased estimator of \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\). Since it is linear in \(\mathbf{y}\), there exists a constant matrix

\[ \mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{(p+1)\times n} \]

such that

\[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}. \]

Define

\[ \mathbf{D} = \mathbf{A} - (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'. \]

Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} &= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{y} \\ &= \left[ \mathbf{A} - (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}' + (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}' \right] \mathbf{y} \\ &= \left[ \mathbf{A} - (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}' \right] \mathbf{y} + (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y} \\ &= \mathbf{D}\mathbf{y} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}. \end{aligned} \]

Since \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is unbiased,

\[ \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] = \boldsymbol{\beta} \qquad \forall\, \boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^{p+1}. \]

On the other hand,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \mathbf{D}\mathbf{y} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \\ &= \mathbf{D} \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] + \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] && \text{lin. exp.} \\ &= \mathbf{D}\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\beta} && \text{mean properties}. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] = \boldsymbol{\beta} \quad \forall\, \boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^{p+1} &\implies \mathbf{D}\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\beta} = \boldsymbol{\beta} \quad \forall\, \boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^{p+1} \\ &\implies \mathbf{D}\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{0} \quad \forall\, \boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^{p+1} \\ &\implies \boxed{ \mathbf{D}\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{0}. } \end{aligned} \]

We now study the covariance matrix of \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\).

Since

\[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{D}\mathbf{y} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}, \]

we have

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{V} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] &= \mathbb{V} \left[ \mathbf{D}\mathbf{y} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \\ &= \mathbb{V} \left[ \mathbf{D}\mathbf{y} + (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y} \right] \\ &= \mathbb{V} \left[ \left( \mathbf{D} + (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}' \right) \mathbf{y} \right] \\ &= \left( \mathbf{D} + (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}' \right) \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}] \left( \mathbf{D}' + \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \right) && \text{var. trans.} \\ &= \sigma^2 \left( \mathbf{D} + (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}' \right) \left( \mathbf{D}' + \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \right) && \text{variance assump.} \\ &= \sigma^2 \Big[ \mathbf{D}\mathbf{D}' + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{X} (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \\ &\qquad\qquad + (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{D}' + (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X} (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \Big] \\ &= \sigma^2 \left[ \mathbf{D}\mathbf{D}' + (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \right] && \text{$\mathbf{D}\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{0}$} \\ &= \sigma^2 \mathbf{D}\mathbf{D}' + \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right]. \end{aligned} \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] - \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] = \sigma^2 \mathbf{D}\mathbf{D}'. } \]

For any vector

\[ \mathbf{a} \in \mathbb{R}^{p+1}, \]

we have

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{a}' \mathbf{D}\mathbf{D}' \mathbf{a} &= (\mathbf{D}'\mathbf{a})' (\mathbf{D}'\mathbf{a}) \\ &= \left\| \mathbf{D}'\mathbf{a} \right\|^2 \\ &\geq 0. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \mathbf{D}\mathbf{D}' \succeq \mathbf{0}, \]

and hence

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] - \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \succeq \mathbf{0}. } \]

The Gauss–Markov theorem is often summarized by saying that the OLS estimator is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator, or BLUE.

Each word has a specific meaning:

  • Linear: \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is a linear function of \(\mathbf{y}\).
  • Unbiased: \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]=\boldsymbol{\beta}\).
  • Best: among all linear unbiased estimators, no competitor has a smaller covariance matrix in the positive-semidefinite ordering.

The meaning of “best” can also be expressed in terms of any linear combination of the coefficient estimators. For any

\[ \mathbf{a} \in \mathbb{R}^{p+1}, \]

the Gauss–Markov result implies

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{V} \left[ \mathbf{a}' \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] - \mathbb{V} \left[ \mathbf{a}' \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] &= \mathbf{a}' \left( \mathbb{V} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] - \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right) \mathbf{a} \\ &\geq 0. \end{aligned} \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V} \left[ \mathbf{a}' \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \leq \mathbb{V} \left[ \mathbf{a}' \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] } \]

for every linear combination of the coefficients.

This result creates an important connection between the deterministic and probabilistic treatments of least squares.

The estimator

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y} \]

was obtained earlier from the optimization problem

\[ \underset{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{\operatorname{minimize}} \; (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})' (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}). \]

That derivation required no assumptions about the mean, variance, or distribution of the errors.

Gauss–Markov establishes something different:

\[ \boxed{ \text{deriving OLS requires no probabilistic assumptions}, } \]

whereas

\[ \boxed{ \text{proving that OLS is BLUE requires the mean and variance assumptions}. } \]

Thus, the progression is

\[ \text{least-squares optimization} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}, \]

followed by

\[ \text{mean and variance assumptions} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{statistical optimality of } \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}. \]

The OLS estimator is therefore not chosen because the probabilistic assumptions were used to construct it. Instead, the probabilistic assumptions allow us to establish important statistical properties of an estimator that was already obtained from an optimization problem.

7.4.0.1 MSE Optimality of OLS

The Gauss–Markov result also has an important consequence when estimator performance is measured using mean squared error.

Definition 7.2 (Mean Squared Error of a Vector Estimator) Let \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) be an estimator of

\[ \boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^{p+1}. \]

The mean squared error (MSE) of \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is defined as

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{MSE} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) = \mathbb{E} \left[ \left\| \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \boldsymbol{\beta} \right\|^2 \right]. } \]

The bias of \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is defined as

\[ \operatorname{Bias} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) = \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] - \boldsymbol{\beta}. \]

The MSE therefore measures the expected squared Euclidean distance between the estimator and the parameter being estimated. It combines two possible sources of estimation error: the variability of the estimator and its bias.

The following proposition makes this decomposition explicit.

Proposition 7.7 (Bias-Variance Decomposition of MSE) For any vector estimator \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) of \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) with finite second moments,

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{MSE} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) = \operatorname{tr} \left( \mathbb{V} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right) + \left\| \operatorname{Bias} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) \right\|^2. } \]

Proof

Proof. First, write the estimation error as

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \boldsymbol{\beta} &= \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] + \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] - \boldsymbol{\beta} \\ &= \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] + \operatorname{Bias} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right). \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{MSE} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \left\| \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \boldsymbol{\beta} \right\|^2 \right] && \text{def. MSE} \\ &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \left\| \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] + \operatorname{Bias} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) \right\|^2 \right] \\ &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \left\| \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right\|^2 \right] \\ &\quad + 2 \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right]' \operatorname{Bias} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) \\ &\quad + \left\| \operatorname{Bias} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) \right\|^2 && \text{exp. sq.} \\ &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \left\| \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right\|^2 \right] + \left\| \operatorname{Bias} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) \right\|^2 && \text{lin. exp.} \end{aligned} \]

since

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right] &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] - \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \\ &= \mathbf{0}. \end{aligned} \]

For the first term,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E} \left[ \left\| \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right\|^2 \right] &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right)' \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right) \right] \\ &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \operatorname{tr} \left( \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right) \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right)' \right) \right] && \text{trace identity} \\ &= \operatorname{tr} \left( \mathbb{E} \left[ \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right) \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbb{E} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right)' \right] \right) && \text{lin. exp.} \\ &= \operatorname{tr} \left( \mathbb{V} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right) && \text{def. variance}. \end{aligned} \]

Consequently,

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{MSE} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) = \operatorname{tr} \left( \mathbb{V} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right) + \left\| \operatorname{Bias} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) \right\|^2. } \]

The proposition shows that MSE consists of two components:

\[ \boxed{ \text{MSE} = \text{total variance} + \text{squared bias}. } \]

Here,

\[ \operatorname{tr} \left( \mathbb{V} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right) \]

is the sum of the variances of the individual components of the estimator, while

\[ \left\| \operatorname{Bias} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) \right\|^2 \]

measures the squared distance between the mean of the estimator and the true parameter vector.

In particular, if \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is unbiased, then

\[ \operatorname{Bias} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) = \mathbf{0}, \]

and therefore

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{MSE} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) = \operatorname{tr} \left( \mathbb{V} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right). } \]

We can now combine this result with the Gauss–Markov theorem.

Proposition 7.8 (MSE Optimality of OLS) Under the assumptions of the Gauss–Markov proposition, let \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) be any linear unbiased estimator of \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\). Then

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{MSE} \left( \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) \leq \operatorname{MSE} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right). } \]

Proof

Proof. Since both \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) are unbiased, the Bias–Variance Decomposition of MSE gives

\[ \operatorname{MSE} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) = \operatorname{tr} \left( \mathbb{V} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right) \]

and

\[ \operatorname{MSE} \left( \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) = \operatorname{tr} \left( \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right). \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{MSE} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) - \operatorname{MSE} \left( \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) &= \operatorname{tr} \left( \mathbb{V} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right) - \operatorname{tr} \left( \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right) \\ &= \operatorname{tr} \left( \mathbb{V} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] - \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right) && \text{lin. tr.} \\ &\geq 0 && \text{Gauss--Markov}. \end{aligned} \]

The last inequality follows because

\[ \mathbb{V} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] - \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \succeq \mathbf{0}, \]

and the trace of a positive-semidefinite matrix is non-negative.

Therefore,

\[ \operatorname{MSE} \left( \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) \leq \operatorname{MSE} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right). \]

Thus, among linear unbiased estimators, OLS is optimal both in the covariance-matrix ordering and under the scalar MSE criterion.

The restriction to linear unbiased estimators remains important. The Gauss–Markov theorem does not imply that OLS minimizes MSE among all possible estimators.

Finally, none of the Gauss–Markov results require a Normal distribution for the errors. The assumptions used are

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] = \mathbf{0} \]

and

\[ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{e}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}, \]

together with a fixed, full-column-rank design matrix.

The role of the next chapter is therefore different. Normality is not introduced to justify OLS as BLUE. Instead, it will allow us to obtain exact sampling distributions and develop inferential procedures.

The progression is

\[ \boxed{ \begin{array}{c} \text{Mean and variance assumptions} \\ \Downarrow \\ \text{unbiasedness, covariance structure, and Gauss--Markov} \\[3mm] \text{Normality assumption} \\ \Downarrow \\ \text{exact sampling distributions and inference}. \end{array} } \]

7.5 Estimation of \(\sigma^2\)

The variance assumption introduced the unknown parameter \(\sigma^2\) through

\[ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{e}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}. \]

We now ask how \(\sigma^2\) can be estimated from the observed regression fit.

Since the residual vector

\[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} = \mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}} \]

measures the part of the response that is not explained by the fitted regression, a natural quantity to use is the residual sum of squares

\[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}}. \]

We therefore consider estimators of the form

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\sigma}^2 = a \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}}, } \]

where \(a\) is a constant scalar.

Our objective is to choose \(a\) so that

\[ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\sigma}^2 \right] = \sigma^2. \]

To determine the appropriate value of \(a\), we first study the expectation of

\[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}}. \]

The following decomposition connects this calculation directly to the projection geometry developed earlier.

Proposition 7.9 (Decomposition of Random Variation) Under the mean assumption,

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}, \]

the following identity holds:

\[ \boxed{ \left( \mathbf{y} - \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] \right)' \left( \mathbf{y} - \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] \right) = \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} + \left( \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}] \right)' \left( \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}] \right). } \]

Proof

Proof. Beginning with the left-hand side,

\[ \begin{aligned} \left( \mathbf{y} - \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] \right)' & \left( \mathbf{y} - \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] \right) \\ &= \left( \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} \right)' \left( \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} \right) && \text{mean response} \\ &= \left( \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} + \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} \right)' \\ &\qquad \left( \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} + \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} \right) \\ &= \left( \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right)' \left( \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) \\ &\quad + 2 \left( \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right)' \left( \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} \right) \\ &\quad + \left( \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} \right)' \left( \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} \right) && \text{exp. sq.} \\ &= \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} + 2 \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \mathbf{X} \left( \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \boldsymbol{\beta} \right) \\ &\quad + \left( \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}] \right)' \left( \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}] \right) \\ &= \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} + \left( \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}] \right)' \left( \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}] \right) && \text{$\hat{\mathbf{e}}'\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{0}'$}. \end{aligned} \]

This identity is the probabilistic counterpart of the geometric decomposition developed in the deterministic treatment of least squares.

The term

\[ \left( \mathbf{y} - \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] \right)' \left( \mathbf{y} - \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] \right) \]

measures the total random variation of the response around its mean.

The decomposition separates this variation into

\[ \boxed{ \text{total random variation} = \text{residual variation} + \text{variation in the fitted component}. } \]

The cross term disappears because of the deterministic least-squares property

\[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{0}'. \]

Thus, the projection geometry developed previously now helps us analyze the expectation of random variation.

The next result determines the expected residual sum of squares.

Proposition 7.10 (Expected Residual Sum of Squares) Suppose that

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] = \mathbf{0} \]

and

\[ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{e}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] = \sigma^2 \left( n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) \right). } \]

If \(\mathbf{X}\) contains an intercept and \(p\) explanatory variables and has full column rank, then

\[ \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = p+1, \]

so

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] = \sigma^2 (n-p-1). } \]

Proof

Proof. From the previous proposition,

\[ \begin{aligned} \left( \mathbf{y} - \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] \right)' \left( \mathbf{y} - \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] \right) &= \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} \\ &\quad+ \left( \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}] \right)' \left( \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}] \right). \end{aligned} \]

Taking expectations,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E} \left[ \left( \mathbf{y} - \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] \right)' \left( \mathbf{y} - \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] \right) \right] &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] \\ &\quad+ \mathbb{E} \left[ \left( \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}] \right)' \left( \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}] \right) \right] && \text{lin. exp.} \end{aligned} \]

For any random vector \(\mathbf{z}\),

\[ \mathbb{E} \left[ \left( \mathbf{z} - \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}] \right)' \left( \mathbf{z} - \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}] \right) \right] = \operatorname{tr} \left( \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{z}] \right). \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{tr} \left( \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}] \right) &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] + \operatorname{tr} \left( \mathbb{V}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}] \right) \\ \sigma^2 \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{I}) &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] + \sigma^2 \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{H}) && \text{variance results} \\ \sigma^2 n &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] + \sigma^2 \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{H}) && \text{idempotent $\mathbf{H}$} \\ &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] + \sigma^2 \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) && \text{rank projection}. \end{aligned} \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] = \sigma^2 \left( n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) \right). } \]

When \(\mathbf{X}\) has full column rank and contains an intercept together with \(p\) explanatory variables,

\[ \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = p+1, \]

and consequently

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] = \sigma^2(n-p-1). } \]

The quantity

\[ n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) \]

has an important geometric interpretation.

Recall that

\[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} = (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{y}, \]

where \(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}\) projects onto

\[ \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})^\perp. \]

Since

\[ \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}) = n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{H}) = n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}), \]

the quantity

\[ \boxed{ n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) } \]

is exactly the dimension of the residual subspace.

This quantity is called the residual degrees of freedom.

For a full-column-rank model with an intercept and \(p\) explanatory variables,

\[ \boxed{ \text{residual degrees of freedom} = n-p-1. } \]

Thus, the degrees-of-freedom correction in the estimator of \(\sigma^2\) is not merely an algebraic adjustment. It is determined by the dimension of the subspace in which the residual vector can vary.

The same expectation can be obtained more directly from the covariance matrix of the residuals.

We already know that

\[ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] = \mathbf{0} \]

and

\[ \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] = \sigma^2 (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}). \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] &= \operatorname{tr} \left( \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] \right) \\ &= \sigma^2 \operatorname{tr} \left( \mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H} \right) \\ &= \sigma^2 \left[ \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{I}) - \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{H}) \right] && \text{lin. tr.} \\ &= \sigma^2 \left[ n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) \right]. \end{aligned} \]

This shorter derivation agrees with the previous result. However, the longer argument reveals more of the structure: the residual degrees of freedom arise from the orthogonal decomposition of the random response into fitted and residual components.

We can now choose the constant \(a\) in

\[ \hat{\sigma}^2 = a \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}}. \]

Proposition 7.11 (Unbiased Estimator of the Error Variance) Consider estimators of the form

\[ \hat{\sigma}^2 = a \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}}, \]

where \(a\) is a constant scalar. Under the mean and variance assumptions,

\[ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\sigma}^2 \right] = \sigma^2 \]

if and only if

\[ \boxed{ a = \frac{1}{ n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) }. } \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} }{ n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) } } \]

is an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\).

For a full-column-rank model with an intercept and \(p\) explanatory variables,

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} }{ n-p-1 }. } \]

Proof

Proof. Since

\[ \hat{\sigma}^2 = a \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}}, \]

we have

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\sigma}^2 \right] &= \mathbb{E} \left[ a \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] \\ &= a \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] && \text{lin. exp.} \\ &= a \sigma^2 \left( n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) \right) && \text{expected RSS}. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\sigma}^2 \right] = \sigma^2 &\iff a \sigma^2 \left( n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) \right) = \sigma^2 \\ &\iff a \left( n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) \right) = 1 \\ &\iff \boxed{ a = \frac{1}{ n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) }. } \end{aligned} \]

Thus, among estimators of the form

\[ a \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}}, \]

with \(a\) a constant scalar, unbiasedness uniquely determines the value of \(a\).

This does not imply that \(\hat{\sigma}^2\) is the only possible unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\). The uniqueness statement applies only within this particular class of estimators.

At this stage, we have established

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\sigma}^2 \right] = \sigma^2. } \]

This conclusion uses only the mean and variance assumptions. We still do not know the probability distribution of \(\hat{\sigma}^2\).

The next chapter will introduce the Normality assumption,

\[ \mathbf{e} \sim N \left( \mathbf{0}, \sigma^2\mathbf{I} \right). \]

Under Normality, the residual sum of squares will satisfy

\[ \boxed{ \frac{ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} }{ \sigma^2 } \sim \chi^2_{ n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) }. } \]

Thus, the progression is

\[ \boxed{ \begin{array}{c} \text{Mean and variance assumptions} \\ \Downarrow \\ \text{unbiased estimation of }\sigma^2 \\[3mm] \text{Normality assumption} \\ \Downarrow \\ \text{sampling distribution of }\hat{\sigma}^2. \end{array} } \]

This completes the development of the first two moments of the linear regression model and prepares the way for exact distributional inference.

7.6 Synthesis

This chapter marks an important change in the interpretation of the linear regression model.

In the previous chapters, the model

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{e} \]

was studied primarily through optimization and linear algebra. Given an observed response vector \(\mathbf{y}\) and design matrix \(\mathbf{X}\), least squares produced

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}, \]

together with the fitted values

\[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{y} \]

and residuals

\[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} = (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{y}. \]

No probabilistic assumptions were needed to obtain these quantities or to establish their geometric properties.

In this chapter, the algebraic form of the model remained unchanged, but its interpretation changed. The response became a random vector generated according to

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{e}, \]

where \(\mathbf{X}\) is treated as fixed, \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is an unknown parameter vector, and \(\mathbf{e}\) represents the random contribution not explicitly represented by the observed explanatory variables.

The first two probabilistic assumptions then determined the first two moments of this model.

The mean assumption,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] = \mathbf{0}, } \]

is equivalent to

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}. } \]

Thus, zero-mean errors and the interpretation of \(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) as the mean response are two equivalent descriptions of the same mean structure.

This assumption also gives the least-squares estimator an important statistical property:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] = \boldsymbol{\beta}. } \]

The quantity that was originally obtained as the solution to an optimization problem is therefore also an unbiased estimator of the parameter vector.

Similarly, the variance assumption,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{e}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}, } \]

is equivalent to

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}. } \]

Under the fixed-design formulation, the response and error vectors differ only by the fixed vector \(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\), so they have exactly the same covariance matrix.

Together, the two assumptions give

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}, \qquad \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}. } \]

These equations specify the first two moments of the response, but they do not specify its complete distribution.

The variance assumption also reveals an important connection between the probabilistic model and the geometry developed previously. The covariance matrices of the principal least-squares quantities are

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] = \sigma^2 (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}, } \]

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] = \sigma^2\mathbf{H}, } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] = \sigma^2(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}). } \]

The matrices \(\mathbf{H}\) and \(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}\) are the same complementary projection matrices that appeared in the deterministic analysis of least squares. Previously, they determined where the fitted values and residuals lie geometrically. Now they also determine how those random quantities vary.

This connection is particularly clear at the observation level:

\[ \mathbb{V}[\hat{y}_i] = \sigma^2h_{ii}, \]

while

\[ \mathbb{V}[\hat{e}_i] = \sigma^2(1-h_{ii}). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V}[\hat{y}_i] + \mathbb{V}[\hat{e}_i] = \sigma^2 = \mathbb{V}[y_i]. } \]

Leverage, which originally arose from the geometry of projection, now also controls how the variability of the response is divided between its fitted and residual components.

The same progression appears in the cross-covariance results. From the deterministic geometry of least squares,

\[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{y}} = 0. \]

Under the variance assumption, we additionally obtain

\[ \mathbb{C} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}, \hat{\mathbf{y}} \right] = \mathbf{0} \]

and

\[ \mathbb{C} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}, \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] = \mathbf{0}. \]

These results should not be confused. The first is a deterministic statement about geometric orthogonality for every fitted dataset. The latter are probabilistic statements about cross-covariances across repeated realizations of the random response.

Thus, one of the central patterns of the chapter is

\[ \boxed{ \text{linear algebra} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{projection geometry} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{probabilistic consequences}. } \]

The Gauss–Markov result provides another important connection between the deterministic and probabilistic viewpoints.

Least squares was derived without probabilistic assumptions. However, once we impose

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}] = \mathbf{0} \]

and

\[ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{e}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}, \]

we can establish that OLS is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator. For every linear unbiased estimator \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\),

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{V} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] - \mathbb{V} \left[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \succeq \mathbf{0}. } \]

Thus, the assumptions are not needed to construct the OLS estimator. They are needed to establish its statistical properties and optimality.

The distinction is

\[ \boxed{ \text{optimization} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}, } \]

followed by

\[ \boxed{ \text{mean and variance assumptions} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{unbiasedness and Gauss--Markov optimality}. } \]

The MSE results reinforce this interpretation. For any vector estimator,

\[ \operatorname{MSE} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) = \operatorname{tr} \left( \mathbb{V} \left[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] \right) + \left\| \operatorname{Bias} \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) \right\|^2. \]

Among linear unbiased estimators, the bias term vanishes, so the Gauss–Markov covariance ordering also implies that OLS minimizes this scalar MSE criterion.

Finally, the chapter introduced the unknown variance parameter \(\sigma^2\) and showed how it can be estimated using the residuals. The key result is

\[ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} \right] = \sigma^2 \left( n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) \right). \]

This leads to the unbiased estimator

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} }{ n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) }. } \]

For a full-column-rank model containing an intercept and \(p\) explanatory variables,

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{ \hat{\mathbf{e}}' \hat{\mathbf{e}} }{ n-p-1 }. } \]

The denominator has a geometric meaning. Since

\[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} = (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{y}, \]

the residual vector lies in

\[ \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})^\perp, \]

whose dimension is

\[ n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}). \]

The residual degrees of freedom therefore arise directly from the dimension of the residual subspace.

At the end of this chapter, we know a substantial amount about the regression model using only assumptions about its first two moments. We have obtained unbiasedness, covariance matrices, zero cross-covariances, Gauss–Markov optimality, and an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\).

What we still do not know are the complete probability distributions of these quantities.

In particular,

\[ \mathbb{C} \left[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}, \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right] = \mathbf{0} \]

does not yet imply independence, and

\[ \mathbb{E} \left[ \hat{\sigma}^2 \right] = \sigma^2 \]

does not determine the sampling distribution of \(\hat{\sigma}^2\).

These limitations explain why the next assumption is needed. The Normality assumption will not be introduced to derive OLS or to establish that it is BLUE. Those results are already available.

Instead, Normality will convert the moment results developed here into exact distributional results:

\[ \boxed{ \begin{array}{c} \text{Mean and variance assumptions} \\ \Downarrow \\ \text{expectations, covariances, unbiasedness, and optimality} \\[3mm] \text{Normality assumption} \\ \Downarrow \\ \text{independence and exact sampling distributions} \\ \Downarrow \\ \text{confidence intervals and hypothesis tests}. \end{array} } \]

The algebra and geometry of least squares therefore remain unchanged throughout this progression. What changes is the amount of probabilistic structure imposed on the data-generating mechanism and, consequently, the strength of the statistical conclusions that can be drawn.
