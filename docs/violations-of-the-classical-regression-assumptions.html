9 Violations of the Classical Regression Assumptions

9.1 Introduction

In Chapters 7 and 8, we studied the statistical properties of the least-squares estimator under a series of assumptions about the data-generating mechanism.

Recall that we began with a linear data-generating mechanism of the form

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \mathbf{u}, \]

where \(\mathbf{X}\) contains the observed explanatory variables, \(\mathbf{Z}\) contains additional explanatory variables that are not included in the regression, and \(\mathbf{u}\) represents other unexplained discrepancies.

By defining the regression errors as

\[ \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \mathbf{u}, \]

we obtained the regression model

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{e}. \]

This representation allowed us to study the statistical properties of the least-squares estimator while recognizing that the observed explanatory variables do not necessarily account for all the variation in the response.

The linearity of the data-generating mechanism will remain an assumption throughout this chapter. In particular, we will continue to interpret \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) as the coefficient vector associated with the observed explanatory variables in this mechanism, without necessarily assigning a causal interpretation to these coefficients.

In Chapter 7, we introduced assumptions about the conditional mean and covariance matrix of the regression errors. In Chapter 8, we imposed an additional distributional assumption, namely that the errors follow a normal distribution.

Together, these assumptions allowed us to establish several properties of the least-squares estimator, including unbiasedness, its covariance matrix, the Gauss–Markov theorem, and the exact distributions used for statistical inference.

However, the assumptions are restrictions on the data-generating mechanism, and there is no guarantee that they will be satisfied in a particular application.

This raises the central question of this chapter:

What happens to the statistical properties of the least-squares estimator when these assumptions are not satisfied?

To answer this question, we will examine the role of each assumption separately, identifying which conclusions remain valid, which no longer follow, and under what additional conditions some of those conclusions can be recovered.

9.1.1 The Mean Assumption

Recall that the least-squares estimator is

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}, \]

provided that \(\mathbf{X}\) has full column rank.

Substituting the regression model, we obtain

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \boldsymbol{\beta} + (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{e}. \]

In Chapter 7, we established that the conditional mean assumption

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}] = \mathbf{0} \]

guarantees that

\[ \mathbb{E}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mid\mathbf{X}] = \boldsymbol{\beta}. \]

Thus, the least-squares estimator is conditionally unbiased for \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\).

However, the more complete data-generating mechanism provides a reason to question this assumption.

Recall that

\[ \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \mathbf{u}. \]

Consequently,

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}\mid\mathbf{X}] \boldsymbol{\gamma} + \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{u}\mid\mathbf{X}]. \]

The mean assumption therefore requires

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}\mid\mathbf{X}] \boldsymbol{\gamma} + \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{u}\mid\mathbf{X}] = \mathbf{0}. \]

This condition imposes a restriction on the relationship between the observed explanatory variables and the unobserved contributions to the response.

In particular, if the unobserved explanatory variables have a systematic relationship with the observed explanatory variables, their contribution to the conditional mean of the errors may not be zero.

If the assumption is violated, the least-squares estimator may no longer be unbiased for the coefficient vector in the data-generating mechanism.

This motivates our first question:

How does a violation of the mean assumption affect least-squares estimation, and under what circumstances can the parameters of the data-generating mechanism still be estimated?

We will investigate the consequences of such violations, including those associated with omitted explanatory variables, and examine conditions under which the resulting estimation problems can be avoided or addressed.

9.1.2 The Variance Assumptions

The assumptions concerning the variance of the regression errors play a different role.

Recall that, under the mean assumption, the conditional covariance matrix of the least-squares estimator is

\[ \mathbb{V}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mid\mathbf{X}] = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}' \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}] \mathbf{X} (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}. \]

In Chapter 7, we assumed that

\[ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}] = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}. \]

This assumption states that the errors have a common conditional variance and are conditionally uncorrelated with one another.

Under this assumption, the covariance matrix of the least-squares estimator simplifies to

\[ \mathbb{V}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mid\mathbf{X}] = \sigma^2(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}. \]

Furthermore, this assumption plays a central role in the Gauss–Markov theorem, which establishes the optimality of least squares within the class of linear unbiased estimators.

However, the decomposition of the regression errors suggests that their covariance structure may be more complicated.

In particular,

\[ \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}] = \mathbb{V}[ \mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\mathbf{u} \mid\mathbf{X} ]. \]

The covariance structure therefore depends on the variation of the omitted explanatory variables, the additional discrepancies, and their relationships with one another.

There is no general reason for this covariance matrix to be a scalar multiple of the identity matrix.

Importantly, violating the variance assumptions does not necessarily invalidate the mean assumption. Consequently, least squares may remain conditionally unbiased even when the classical covariance assumption fails.

Nevertheless, the covariance matrix of the estimator, its efficiency, and the validity of conventional estimates of uncertainty may be affected.

This motivates our second question:

What happens to the precision and efficiency of the least-squares estimator when the errors have unequal variances or are correlated?

We will examine the consequences of relaxing the covariance assumptions and investigate the conditions under which the resulting difficulties can be addressed.

9.1.3 The Normality Assumption

In Chapter 8, we introduced the normality assumption

\[ \mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X} \sim N(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2\mathbf{I}). \]

This assumption allowed us to extend the results obtained in Chapter 7.

In particular, because the least-squares estimator is a linear transformation of the response vector, we established that

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mid\mathbf{X} \sim N\left( \boldsymbol{\beta}, \sigma^2(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \right). \]

Together with the distribution of the residual sum of squares and the independence properties established under normality, this result provided the foundation for the exact confidence intervals and hypothesis tests developed in Chapter 8.

However, the normality assumption is not necessary to establish the expectation or covariance matrix of the least-squares estimator.

Those properties were obtained in Chapter 7 using assumptions only about the first two conditional moments of the regression errors.

Consequently, if the errors are not normally distributed but the mean and variance assumptions remain satisfied, the least-squares estimator retains its conditional unbiasedness and covariance matrix.

What may no longer hold are the exact sampling distributions used for statistical inference.

This motivates our third question:

Can we obtain valid statistical inference without assuming that the regression errors follow a normal distribution?

We will investigate whether the behavior of the least-squares estimator as the sample size increases can provide an alternative justification for confidence intervals and hypothesis tests.

This will require us to study the conditions under which the estimator converges to the parameter being estimated and its sampling distribution can be approximated by a normal distribution.

9.1.4 Objectives of the Chapter

The preceding discussion illustrates that the assumptions introduced in Chapters 7 and 8 serve different purposes.

The mean assumption concerns the relationship between the observed explanatory variables and the unobserved contributions to the response. It plays a central role in establishing the unbiasedness of the least-squares estimator.

The variance assumptions determine the covariance structure of the estimator and provide conditions under which least squares is optimal among linear unbiased estimators.

The normality assumption allows us to obtain exact sampling distributions and construct statistical inference procedures based on them.

These conclusions are related, but they do not all require the same assumptions.

For example, an estimator can remain unbiased when the errors have unequal variances. Similarly, the absence of normally distributed errors does not necessarily prevent consistent estimation or valid large-sample inference.

The objective of this chapter is therefore not to abandon the linear regression framework when its classical assumptions fail. Instead, we will use the mathematical results already established to determine precisely what changes when the assumptions are weakened.

We will proceed by examining violations of the mean assumptions, followed by violations of the variance assumptions, and finally violations of the normality assumption.

In each case, the emphasis will be on understanding the mathematical consequences of the violation. Alternative estimation and inference procedures will be considered when they arise naturally from these consequences.

This approach will allow us to distinguish among the assumptions required for unbiasedness, consistency, efficiency, and statistical inference, and to understand more precisely the limitations and applicability of the classical linear regression model.

9.2 Violations of the Mean Assumption

In Chapter 7, we established that the assumption

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}] = \mathbf{0} \]

guarantees the conditional unbiasedness of the least-squares estimator.

We also established that this assumption allows us to interpret \(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) as the conditional mean of the response:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}] = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}. \]

We now investigate what happens when this assumption is not satisfied.

Throughout this section, we continue to assume that the data-generating mechanism is linear in its explanatory variables and coefficients. In particular,

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \mathbf{u}, \]

where \(\mathbf{X}\) contains the observed explanatory variables, \(\mathbf{Z}\) contains additional explanatory variables omitted from the regression, and \(\mathbf{u}\) represents other unexplained discrepancies.

As before, we define

\[ \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \mathbf{u}, \]

so that

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{e}. \]

The linearity of the complete data-generating mechanism does not guarantee that the mean assumption is satisfied. This depends on the conditional distributions of the components that have been combined into the regression errors.

Our objective is to determine how a nonzero conditional mean of the errors affects the statistical properties of the least-squares estimator.

9.2.1 Consequences for the Conditional Mean of the Response

Let

\[ \boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X}) = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}] \]

denote the conditional mean of the regression errors.

We use this notation to distinguish the conditional mean from the random error vector itself.

From the regression model,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}] &= \mathbb{E}[ \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{e} \mid\mathbf{X} ] \\ &= \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}] \\ &= \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X}). \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}] = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X}). } \]

Under the mean assumption, the second component disappears, and \(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) represents the conditional mean of the response.

When the assumption fails, the errors contain an additional systematic contribution to the conditional mean.

Notice that the word systematic refers here to a contribution whose conditional expectation is not zero. It does not imply that this contribution is necessarily deterministic or that the errors contain no additional randomness.

In particular, we can always decompose the errors as

\[ \mathbf{e} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X}) + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \]

where

\[ \boldsymbol{\epsilon} = \mathbf{e} - \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}]. \]

By construction,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\mid\mathbf{X}] &= \mathbb{E}[ \mathbf{e} - \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}] \mid\mathbf{X} ] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}] - \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}] \\ &= \mathbf{0}. \end{aligned} \]

The response can therefore be expressed as

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{y} = \underbrace{ \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X}) }_{\text{conditional mean}} + \underbrace{ \boldsymbol{\epsilon} }_{\text{zero conditional mean}}. } \]

This representation is always possible when the conditional expectations exist.

However, it does not imply that the original coefficient vector \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) can be recovered from the observed explanatory variables. The additional conditional mean contribution may be unknown and may be related to \(\mathbf{X}\).

Furthermore, even though the complete data-generating mechanism is linear in \(\mathbf{X}\) and \(\mathbf{Z}\), the function \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X})\) need not be linear in \(\mathbf{X}\).

Thus, violating the mean assumption may change the conditional mean represented by the reduced regression model.

9.2.2 Consequences for the Least-Squares Estimator

Recall that the least-squares estimator satisfies

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \boldsymbol{\beta} + (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{e}. \]

The conditional expectation of this estimator is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \mid\mathbf{X} ] &= \boldsymbol{\beta} + (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}' \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}] \\ &= \boldsymbol{\beta} + (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}' \boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X}). \end{aligned} \]

Consequently, the conditional bias of the least-squares estimator is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E}[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \mid\mathbf{X} ] - \boldsymbol{\beta} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}' \boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X}). } \]

This expression provides an exact characterization of the consequences of violating the mean assumption.

In particular, notice that a nonzero conditional mean of the errors does not necessarily imply that the least-squares estimator is biased.

The following proposition establishes the precise condition.

Proposition 9.1 (Conditional Unbiasedness Without the Mean Assumption) Consider the regression model

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{e}, \]

where \(\mathbf{X}\) may be random and has full column rank with probability one.

Suppose that the relevant conditional expectations exist, and define

\[ \boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X}) = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}]. \]

Then the least-squares estimator is conditionally unbiased for \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) if and only if

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{X}'\boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{0}. } \]

Equivalently,

\[ \mathbb{E}[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \mid\mathbf{X} ] = \boldsymbol{\beta} \quad\iff\quad \mathbf{X}' \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}] = \mathbf{0}. \]

Proof

Proof. Using the expression for the conditional expectation of the least-squares estimator,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \mid\mathbf{X} ] = \boldsymbol{\beta} &\iff (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}' \boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{0} && \text{OLS expectation} \\ &\iff \mathbf{X}' \boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{0} && \text{full rank}. \end{aligned} \]

The equivalence follows because \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\) is invertible.

The proposition shows that the mean assumption

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}] = \mathbf{0} \]

is sufficient, but not necessary, for conditional unbiasedness of least squares.

The weaker condition requires only that the conditional mean of the errors be orthogonal to every column of the design matrix.

This distinction is important.

The mean assumption establishes that \(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is the conditional mean of the response. The weaker orthogonality condition establishes that least squares is conditionally unbiased for \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\).

These are different conclusions, and they do not require exactly the same assumptions.

9.2.2.1 A Geometric Interpretation

The previous result can be interpreted using the projection matrices introduced in Chapter 5.

Recall that the hat matrix is

\[ \mathbf{H} = \mathbf{X} (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'. \]

The conditional expectation of the fitted response is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} \mid\mathbf{X} ] &= \mathbb{E}[ \mathbf{H}\mathbf{y} \mid\mathbf{X} ] \\ &= \mathbf{H} \left( \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X}) \right) \\ &= \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{H}\boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X}). \end{aligned} \]

Similarly, because

\[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} = (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{y}, \]

we obtain

\[ \mathbb{E}[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} \mid\mathbf{X} ] = (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}) \boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X}). \]

Thus, the systematic component of the errors can be decomposed as

\[ \boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X}) = \underbrace{ \mathbf{H}\boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X}) }_{\text{component in }\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})} + \underbrace{ (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}) \boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X}) }_{\text{component orthogonal to }\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})}. \]

These two components have different consequences.

The component in the column space of \(\mathbf{X}\) changes the conditional expectation of the fitted coefficients and fitted responses.

The component orthogonal to the column space does not affect the conditional expectation of the coefficient estimates, but it remains in the conditional expectation of the residuals.

In particular, if

\[ \boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X}) \in \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}), \]

then

\[ (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}) \boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{0}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \mathbb{E}[ \hat{\mathbf{e}} \mid\mathbf{X} ] = \mathbf{0}, \]

even though the mean assumption may be violated and the coefficient estimates may be biased.

This shows that the regression residuals do not necessarily reveal a violation of the mean assumption.

The result also reinforces the distinction between regression errors and least-squares residuals. The errors belong to the data-generating mechanism, while the residuals are obtained by projecting the observed response onto the orthogonal complement of the design matrix.

9.2.3 Interpretation Through the Data-Generating Mechanism

We now return to the decomposition introduced in Chapter 7:

\[ \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \mathbf{u}. \]

Taking conditional expectations,

\[ \boldsymbol{\mu}_e(\mathbf{X}) = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}\mid\mathbf{X}] \boldsymbol{\gamma} + \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{u}\mid\mathbf{X}]. \]

Substituting this expression into the conditional bias of least squares gives

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} & \mathbb{E}[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \mid\mathbf{X} ] - \boldsymbol{\beta} \\ &\quad= (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}' \left( \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}\mid\mathbf{X}] \boldsymbol{\gamma} + \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{u}\mid\mathbf{X}] \right). \end{aligned} } \]

The expression illustrates how the statistical properties of least squares depend on the unobserved components of the data-generating mechanism.

Suppose, for example, that

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{u}\mid\mathbf{X}] = \mathbf{0}. \]

Then

\[ \mathbb{E}[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \mid\mathbf{X} ] - \boldsymbol{\beta} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}' \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}\mid\mathbf{X}] \boldsymbol{\gamma}. \]

The conditional bias now depends on the contribution of the omitted explanatory variables to the response and their conditional relationship with the observed explanatory variables.

If

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}\mid\mathbf{X}] \boldsymbol{\gamma} = \mathbf{0}, \]

the mean assumption is satisfied under the additional assumption about \(\mathbf{u}\).

However, this is not the only circumstance under which the coefficient estimates can be conditionally unbiased. As established earlier, it is sufficient that

\[ \mathbf{X}' \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}\mid\mathbf{X}] \boldsymbol{\gamma} = \mathbf{0}. \]

The distinction is between the existence of a systematic omitted contribution and whether that contribution affects the coefficient estimates through the observed design matrix.

9.2.3.1 Example: An Omitted Explanatory Variable

Consider the data-generating mechanism

\[ Y_i = \beta_0+\beta_1X_i+\gamma Z_i+U_i, \]

where \(X_i\) is observed and \(Z_i\) is omitted from the regression.

Suppose that

\[ \mathbb{E}[U_i\mid\mathbf{X}] = 0, \]

and that the conditional mean of the omitted variable satisfies

\[ \mathbb{E}[Z_i\mid\mathbf{X}] = a+\delta X_i, \]

where \(\mathbf{X}\) contains an intercept column and the observed values \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\).

Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[Y_i\mid\mathbf{X}] &= \beta_0+\beta_1X_i + \gamma\mathbb{E}[Z_i\mid\mathbf{X}] \\ &= \beta_0+\beta_1X_i + \gamma(a+\delta X_i) \\ &= (\beta_0+\gamma a) + (\beta_1+\gamma\delta)X_i. \end{aligned} \]

Notice that the conditional mean remains linear in the observed explanatory variable.

However, its coefficients are

\[ \beta_0^*=\beta_0+\gamma a \]

and

\[ \boxed{ \beta_1^*=\beta_1+\gamma\delta. } \]

Therefore, when we estimate a simple linear regression using only \(X_i\), its slope estimator satisfies

\[ \mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_1\mid\mathbf{X}] = \beta_1+\gamma\delta. \]

Consequently,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_1\mid\mathbf{X}] - \beta_1 = \gamma\delta. } \]

This result illustrates two distinct interpretations.

Relative to the coefficient \(\beta_1\) in the complete data-generating mechanism, the least-squares slope estimator is conditionally biased whenever \(\gamma\delta\neq0\).

However, relative to the coefficient \(\beta_1^*\) in the conditional mean of the response given \(X_i\), the same estimator is conditionally unbiased.

The estimator itself has not changed. What changes is the parameter that the reduced regression represents.

This is an important consequence of omitting explanatory variables: the resulting regression can correctly represent the conditional mean of the response while its coefficients differ from those associated with the observed explanatory variables in the complete data-generating mechanism.

Furthermore, the coefficients in this complete mechanism do not necessarily have a causal interpretation. Establishing causality would require additional assumptions beyond the linearity of the model.

9.2.4 When Can the Mean Assumption Be Justified?

The previous results identify the mathematical consequences of a nonzero conditional mean of the errors. They do not, by themselves, explain whether the mean assumption should be expected to hold.

That depends on how the explanatory variables and the unobserved contributions arise in the data-generating mechanism.

One possibility is that the observed explanatory variables are related to the omitted explanatory variables. Another is that the additional discrepancies represented by \(\mathbf{u}\) are associated with the observed explanatory variables.

In either case, the conditional mean of the errors may not be zero.

This suggests that the validity of the mean assumption cannot generally be established from the least-squares optimization problem itself. It depends on additional information about the mechanism generating the explanatory variables and the response.

A useful illustration arises when one of the explanatory variables is assigned randomly.

9.2.4.1 Example: Random Assignment of an Explanatory Variable

Consider the model

\[ Y_i = \beta_0+\tau D_i + \mathbf{z}_i'\boldsymbol{\gamma} + u_i, \]

where \(D_i\) indicates whether individual \(i\) receives a treatment and \(\mathbf{z}_i\) contains additional pre-treatment characteristics.

Suppose initially that the individuals and their characteristics are fixed, while their treatment assignments are random.

If we estimate a regression using only an intercept and \(D_i\), the least-squares coefficient of treatment is

\[ \hat{\tau} = \bar{Y}_1-\bar{Y}_0, \]

where \(\bar{Y}_1\) and \(\bar{Y}_0\) are the observed means of the treatment and control groups.

Using the complete data-generating mechanism, this difference becomes

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\tau} &= \left( \beta_0+\tau + \overline{\mathbf{z}}_1' \boldsymbol{\gamma} + \bar{u}_1 \right) - \left( \beta_0 + \overline{\mathbf{z}}_0' \boldsymbol{\gamma} + \bar{u}_0 \right) \\ &= \tau + (\overline{\mathbf{z}}_1- \overline{\mathbf{z}}_0)' \boldsymbol{\gamma} + (\bar{u}_1-\bar{u}_0). \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\tau}-\tau = (\overline{\mathbf{z}}_1- \overline{\mathbf{z}}_0)' \boldsymbol{\gamma} + (\bar{u}_1-\bar{u}_0). } \]

This expression identifies the contributions of the omitted characteristics and discrepancies to the estimation error.

Now suppose that exactly \(n_1\) individuals are assigned to treatment and \(n_0\) to control through complete randomization, with both group sizes positive.

If the omitted characteristics and discrepancies are fixed before assignment, each individual has the same probability of being included in either group.

Consequently, taking expectations over the possible assignments,

\[ \mathbb{E}_D[ \overline{\mathbf{z}}_1 ] = \mathbb{E}_D[ \overline{\mathbf{z}}_0 ] = \overline{\mathbf{z}}, \]

and

\[ \mathbb{E}_D[\bar{u}_1] = \mathbb{E}_D[\bar{u}_0] = \bar{u}. \]

It follows that

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}_D[\hat{\tau}-\tau] &= \mathbb{E}_D[ \overline{\mathbf{z}}_1- \overline{\mathbf{z}}_0 ]' \boldsymbol{\gamma} + \mathbb{E}_D[ \bar{u}_1-\bar{u}_0 ] \\ &= \mathbf{0}'\boldsymbol{\gamma} + 0 \\ &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E}_D[\hat{\tau}]=\tau. } \]

Notice that the equality holds over the random assignment mechanism. It does not require the realized treatment and control groups to have identical characteristics.

The example shows how the assignment mechanism can eliminate systematic differences between the treatment and control groups in expectation, even when relevant explanatory variables are omitted from the fitted regression.

It is also important to distinguish this randomization-based result from the conditional unbiasedness results established earlier. Here, the expectation is taken over the assignments themselves, rather than conditional on the complete realized design matrix.

If instead the explanatory variables are modeled jointly as random variables, random assignment independent of the omitted pre-treatment characteristics can provide a justification for the corresponding conditional mean assumption, after accounting for any constant contribution through the intercept.

Thus, random assignment provides one possible explanation for why a relevant orthogonality or mean condition may hold. It is not a necessary condition, nor does it guarantee that every explanatory variable in a more general regression satisfies the mean assumption.

9.2.5 Implications for Estimation

The preceding results establish that a violation of the mean assumption does not have a single universal consequence.

If

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}] \neq \mathbf{0}, \]

then \(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) no longer represents the complete conditional mean of the response.

However, the least-squares estimator remains conditionally unbiased for \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) whenever

\[ \mathbf{X}' \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}] = \mathbf{0}. \]

If this weaker condition also fails, the coefficient estimator has conditional bias, whose magnitude and direction are determined by

\[ (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}' \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}]. \]

In this case, the regression coefficients obtained through least squares need not estimate the coefficients associated with the observed explanatory variables in the complete data-generating mechanism.

Additional information about that mechanism may allow us to address the problem.

For example, if the relevant omitted variables become observable, we may be able to include them in the regression model. However, this requires appropriate conditions on the enlarged model and does not automatically guarantee that its errors satisfy the mean assumption.

Random assignment provides another possibility in experimental settings, as illustrated above.

In some observational settings, additional variables may provide information about the explanatory variables of interest while satisfying moment conditions involving the regression errors. This motivates instrumental-variable estimation, which replaces the problematic relationship between the original explanatory variables and the errors with alternative identifying conditions.

These approaches are different ways of addressing a common mathematical problem: the information provided by the observed explanatory variables may be insufficient to recover the coefficient vector in the complete data-generating mechanism using ordinary least squares.

The important conclusion is that a violation of the mean assumption must be understood in relation to the parameter being estimated and the information available about the data-generating mechanism.

We will now turn to the variance assumptions. Unlike the mean assumption, violations of these assumptions need not affect the conditional expectation of the least-squares estimator. Instead, they principally change its covariance structure and the conclusions that can be drawn about its precision and efficiency.

9.2.6 Instrumental Variables and Alternative Orthogonality Conditions

We have established that the conditional mean assumption

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}] = \mathbf{0} \]

is sufficient to guarantee the conditional unbiasedness of the least-squares estimator.

However, we also established that this assumption is stronger than necessary. In particular, conditional unbiasedness requires only that

\[ \mathbf{X}'\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}] = \mathbf{0}. \]

More generally, when the design matrix is random, the statistical properties of an estimator can be studied using expectations over the joint distribution of the explanatory variables and errors.

For example, the population orthogonality condition

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_i e_i] = \mathbf{0} \]

is weaker than the zero conditional mean assumption, where \(\mathbf{x}_i'\) denotes the \(i\)th row of the design matrix.

Under suitable sampling and regularity conditions, this orthogonality condition is sufficient for the consistency of least squares, even when the stronger conditional mean assumption does not hold.

What happens if this weaker orthogonality condition is also violated?

To answer this question, recall the linear data-generating mechanism

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \mathbf{u}, \]

where \(\mathbf{Z}\) contains explanatory variables omitted from the regression.

By defining

\[ \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \mathbf{u}, \]

we obtain

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{e}. \]

The least-squares estimator satisfies

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{OLS}} - \boldsymbol{\beta} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{e}. \]

If the explanatory variables are systematically associated with the errors, the second term may not have expectation zero and may not disappear as the sample size increases.

Consequently, least squares may fail to recover \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\).

This does not necessarily imply that \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) cannot be estimated from the available information. It means that the particular relationship between \(\mathbf{X}\) and \(\mathbf{e}\) used by ordinary least squares may not provide a suitable basis for estimation.

Suppose that we observe another set of variables, represented by a matrix \(\mathbf{W}\), which are related to the explanatory variables in \(\mathbf{X}\) but satisfy an appropriate orthogonality condition with the regression errors.

Can these additional variables provide enough information to estimate \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\)?

This is the question underlying instrumental-variable estimation.

9.2.6.1 Identification Using Alternative Variables

We begin with a single explanatory variable to illustrate the main idea.

Suppose that the data-generating mechanism is

\[ Y_i = \beta_0+\beta_1X_i+e_i, \]

where \(X_i\) and \(e_i\) may be correlated.

Assume that the relevant second moments exist and that

\[ \mathbb{E}[e_i]=0. \]

Taking covariance with \(X_i\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{C}[X_i,Y_i] &= \mathbb{C}[ X_i,\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+e_i ] \\ &= \beta_1\mathbb{V}[X_i] + \mathbb{C}[X_i,e_i]. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{\mathbb{C}[X_i,Y_i]} {\mathbb{V}[X_i]} = \beta_1 + \frac{\mathbb{C}[X_i,e_i]} {\mathbb{V}[X_i]}. \]

The left-hand side is the slope of the population linear projection of \(Y_i\) on \(X_i\).

If

\[ \mathbb{C}[X_i,e_i]\neq0, \]

this slope differs from \(\beta_1\).

Now suppose that we observe another variable \(W_i\) satisfying

\[ \mathbb{C}[W_i,e_i]=0. \]

Taking covariance with \(W_i\) instead gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{C}[W_i,Y_i] &= \mathbb{C}[ W_i,\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+e_i ] \\ &= \beta_1\mathbb{C}[W_i,X_i] + \mathbb{C}[W_i,e_i] \\ &= \beta_1\mathbb{C}[W_i,X_i]. \end{aligned} \]

If

\[ \mathbb{C}[W_i,X_i]\neq0, \]

we can solve for \(\beta_1\):

\[ \boxed{ \beta_1 = \frac{\mathbb{C}[W_i,Y_i]} {\mathbb{C}[W_i,X_i]}. } \]

Thus, even when the relationship between \(X_i\) and the regression error prevents the population regression slope from recovering \(\beta_1\), another observed variable may allow us to identify this parameter.

The variable \(W_i\) is called an instrumental variable, or an instrument.

Notice that two conditions were necessary:

  1. The instrument must be uncorrelated with the regression error,

    \[ \mathbb{C}[W_i,e_i]=0. \]

  2. The instrument must be correlated with the explanatory variable,

    \[ \mathbb{C}[W_i,X_i]\neq0. \]

The first condition is commonly called instrument exogeneity, while the second is called instrument relevance.

These conditions serve different purposes.

Exogeneity ensures that the relationship between the instrument and the response does not contain a contribution from the regression error.

Relevance ensures that this relationship provides information about the coefficient \(\beta_1\).

If the instrument is unrelated to \(X_i\), then its relationship with \(Y_i\) cannot identify \(\beta_1\) through the preceding equation.

Importantly, the instrument does not need to be independent of the explanatory variable. On the contrary, some relationship between them is required.

9.2.6.2 Interpretation Through the Data-Generating Mechanism

Recall that the regression error can be decomposed as

\[ e_i = \mathbf{z}_i'\boldsymbol{\gamma} + u_i, \]

where \(\mathbf{z}_i\) contains omitted explanatory variables.

Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{C}[W_i,e_i] &= \mathbb{C}[ W_i,\mathbf{z}_i'\boldsymbol{\gamma}+u_i ] \\ &= \mathbb{C}[W_i,\mathbf{z}_i]' \boldsymbol{\gamma} + \mathbb{C}[W_i,u_i]. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore, the instrument exogeneity condition requires

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{C}[W_i,\mathbf{z}_i]' \boldsymbol{\gamma} + \mathbb{C}[W_i,u_i] = 0. } \]

This provides a direct interpretation of instrument validity within the data-generating mechanism established in Chapter 7.

For example, a sufficient condition is that the instrument be uncorrelated with both the omitted explanatory variables and the additional discrepancies:

\[ \mathbb{C}[W_i,\mathbf{z}_i]=\mathbf{0}, \qquad \mathbb{C}[W_i,u_i]=0. \]

However, these separate conditions are stronger than necessary. Only the covariance with their combined contribution to the response must be zero.

The mathematical possibility of finding such a variable does not guarantee that one exists in a particular application.

Furthermore, instrument exogeneity is an assumption about the data-generating mechanism. A correlation between the instrument and the observed explanatory variable can be investigated using the data, but the absence of a relationship between the instrument and the unobserved regression error cannot generally be verified directly.

Thus, instrumental-variable estimation replaces one problematic assumption with another identifying assumption. It does not eliminate the need for assumptions about the data-generating mechanism.

9.2.6.3 Estimation from a Random Sample

The previous argument establishes how an instrument can identify the parameter \(\beta_1\) at the population level.

We now consider how to estimate this parameter from observed data.

Suppose that we observe \(n\) independent and identically distributed realizations of \((Y_i,X_i,W_i)\).

The population relationship

\[ \beta_1 = \frac{\mathbb{C}[W_i,Y_i]} {\mathbb{C}[W_i,X_i]} \]

suggests replacing the population covariances with their sample counterparts.

This gives

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\beta}_{1,\mathrm{IV}} = \frac{ \sum_{i=1}^n (W_i-\bar{W})(Y_i-\bar{Y}) }{ \sum_{i=1}^n (W_i-\bar{W})(X_i-\bar{X}) }, } \]

provided that the denominator is nonzero.

The corresponding intercept estimator is

\[ \hat{\beta}_{0,\mathrm{IV}} = \bar{Y} - \hat{\beta}_{1,\mathrm{IV}}\bar{X}. \]

Compare this estimator with the ordinary least-squares slope:

\[ \hat{\beta}_{1,\mathrm{OLS}} = \frac{ \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\bar{X})(Y_i-\bar{Y}) }{ \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\bar{X})^2 }. \]

Both estimators have a similar mathematical structure.

Least squares uses the observed explanatory variable itself to establish the relationship with the response.

Instrumental-variable estimation instead uses another variable whose relationship with the regression error is assumed to satisfy the required orthogonality condition.

The estimator is not necessarily unbiased in a finite sample. Its denominator is random, and taking the expectation of a ratio does not generally produce the ratio of the expectations.

However, under appropriate conditions, the estimator is consistent.

Proposition 9.2 (Consistency of the Instrumental-Variable Estimator) Suppose that \((Y_i,X_i,W_i)\), \(i=1,\ldots,n\), are independent and identically distributed observations satisfying

\[ Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+e_i. \]

Assume that the relevant second moments are finite and that

\[ \mathbb{C}[W_i,e_i]=0, \]

and

\[ \mathbb{C}[W_i,X_i]\neq0. \]

Then

\[ \hat{\beta}_{1,\mathrm{IV}} \xrightarrow{p} \beta_1. \]

Proof

Proof. Recall that

\[ \hat{\beta}_{1,\mathrm{IV}} = \frac{ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (W_i-\bar{W})(Y_i-\bar{Y}) }{ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (W_i-\bar{W})(X_i-\bar{X}) }. \]

By the law of large numbers and the continuous mapping theorem,

\[ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (W_i-\bar{W})(Y_i-\bar{Y}) \xrightarrow{p} \mathbb{C}[W_i,Y_i], \]

and

\[ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (W_i-\bar{W})(X_i-\bar{X}) \xrightarrow{p} \mathbb{C}[W_i,X_i]. \]

Because

\[ \mathbb{C}[W_i,X_i]\neq0, \]

we obtain

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\beta}_{1,\mathrm{IV}} &\xrightarrow{p} \frac{ \mathbb{C}[W_i,Y_i] }{ \mathbb{C}[W_i,X_i] } \\ &= \frac{ \beta_1\mathbb{C}[W_i,X_i] + \mathbb{C}[W_i,e_i] }{ \mathbb{C}[W_i,X_i] } \\ &= \beta_1. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \hat{\beta}_{1,\mathrm{IV}} \xrightarrow{p} \beta_1. \]

The proposition shows that consistency can be established without requiring

\[ \mathbb{E}[e_i\mid X_i]=0. \]

Instead, we use a different population orthogonality condition involving the instrument.

This illustrates the broader distinction between finite-sample unbiasedness and consistency. The instrumental-variable estimator may not be unbiased for every sample size, while still converging to the parameter of interest as the sample size increases.

9.2.7 Multiple Explanatory Variables and Projection Geometry

We now consider the multiple regression model

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{e}, \]

where \(\mathbf{X}\) is an \(n\times p\) design matrix and \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) contains \(p\) unknown coefficients.

Suppose that the observed explanatory variables do not satisfy the orthogonality conditions required for consistent least-squares estimation.

Instead, we observe an \(n\times q\) matrix \(\mathbf{W}\) containing \(q\) instruments.

We will assume that \(q\geq p\) and that \(\mathbf{W}\) has full column rank. When the regression contains an intercept, the instrument matrix also includes an intercept column.

The instrumental-variable approach is based on the population moment condition

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}_i e_i] = \mathbf{0}, } \]

where \(\mathbf{w}_i'\) is the \(i\)th row of \(\mathbf{W}\).

Since

\[ e_i = y_i-\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}, \]

this condition becomes

\[ \mathbb{E}[ \mathbf{w}_i (y_i-\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}) ] = \mathbf{0}. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}_i y_i] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}_i\mathbf{x}_i'] \boldsymbol{\beta}. \]

This is a system of \(q\) equations involving the \(p\) unknown coefficients.

The parameter vector can be uniquely identified from these population moments when the matrix

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}_i\mathbf{x}_i'] \]

has full column rank \(p\).

Thus, the scalar relevance condition from the previous example generalizes to a rank condition.

The instruments must collectively provide enough independent information to identify the coefficients of the observed explanatory variables.

9.2.7.1 From Population Orthogonality to Sample Estimation

The population moment condition suggests the sample relationship

\[ \mathbf{W}' (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}) \approx \mathbf{0}. \]

If \(q=p\) and the matrix \(\mathbf{W}'\mathbf{X}\) is invertible, we can impose the corresponding sample equations exactly:

\[ \mathbf{W}' (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = \mathbf{0}. \]

Solving,

\[ \mathbf{W}'\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{W}'\mathbf{X} \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}, \]

so that

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{IV}} = (\mathbf{W}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{W}'\mathbf{y}. } \]

This is the exactly identified instrumental-variable estimator.

If \(q>p\), however, there are more sample equations than unknown coefficients. In general, no coefficient vector will satisfy all the equations simultaneously.

We therefore need a criterion for obtaining a solution that approximately satisfies the sample orthogonality conditions.

The projection geometry developed in Chapter 5 provides a natural construction.

9.2.7.2 Two-Stage Least Squares

Recall that the orthogonal projection matrix onto the column space of \(\mathbf{W}\) is

\[ \mathbf{P}_W = \mathbf{W} (\mathbf{W}'\mathbf{W})^{-1} \mathbf{W}'. \]

This matrix is symmetric and idempotent:

\[ \mathbf{P}_W'=\mathbf{P}_W, \qquad \mathbf{P}_W^2=\mathbf{P}_W. \]

Consider the following objective function:

\[ S_{\mathrm{IV}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})' \mathbf{P}_W (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}). \]

Because \(\mathbf{P}_W\) is symmetric and idempotent,

\[ \begin{aligned} S_{\mathrm{IV}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) &= (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})' \mathbf{P}_W'\mathbf{P}_W (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}) \\ &= \left\| \mathbf{P}_W (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}) \right\|^2. \end{aligned} \]

Thus, rather than minimizing the entire residual vector as ordinary least squares does, we minimize the squared length of its projection onto the instrument space.

Differentiating,

\[ \frac{\partial S_{\mathrm{IV}}} {\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}} = -2\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}). \]

Setting the derivative equal to zero gives

\[ \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{X} \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{y}. \]

Provided that \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{X}\) is invertible,

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{2SLS}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{y}. } \]

This is the two-stage least-squares estimator, commonly abbreviated as 2SLS.

The expression resembles the ordinary least-squares estimator, but the projection matrix \(\mathbf{P}_W\) changes which components of the observed data determine the coefficient estimates.

Notice that the estimation procedure is again based on projection matrices, rather than requiring a completely different algebraic framework.

9.2.7.3 Why Is It Called Two-Stage Least Squares?

The estimator can be obtained through two ordinary least-squares operations.

First, regress each column of \(\mathbf{X}\) on the instrument matrix \(\mathbf{W}\).

The matrix of fitted explanatory variables is

\[ \hat{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{X}. \]

This operation retains the components of the explanatory variables that belong to the column space generated by the instruments.

Second, regress \(\mathbf{y}\) on the fitted explanatory variables \(\hat{\mathbf{X}}\).

The resulting coefficient estimator is

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\hat{\mathbf{X}}'\hat{\mathbf{X}})^{-1} \hat{\mathbf{X}}'\mathbf{y}. \]

Substituting

\[ \hat{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{X}, \]

we obtain

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} &= (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W' \mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W'\mathbf{y} \\ &= (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{y} && \text{symmetric, idempotent} \\ &= \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{2SLS}}. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore, the two-stage procedure produces exactly the estimator obtained by minimizing the projected residual sum of squares.

The geometric interpretation is particularly useful.

Ordinary least squares uses the entire column space of \(\mathbf{X}\) to determine the fitted coefficients.

Two-stage least squares first projects the explanatory variables onto the instrument space and then uses these projected components to estimate the coefficients.

In this sense, the procedure replaces the original explanatory-variable directions with directions generated from the instruments.

However, this construction is statistically useful only when the instruments satisfy the required exogeneity and relevance conditions.

The projection itself does not establish instrument validity.

Furthermore, although the coefficient estimates can be computed through two ordinary least-squares regressions, their standard errors cannot generally be obtained by treating the second regression as an ordinary linear regression with fixed fitted predictors and classical errors. The uncertainty of the estimator must be derived from the original model and the instrumental-variable estimation procedure.

9.2.8 Statistical Properties of Two-Stage Least Squares

The two-stage least-squares estimator satisfies

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{2SLS}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{y}. \]

Substituting the regression model,

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{2SLS}} &= (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W (\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\mathbf{e}) \\ &= \boldsymbol{\beta} + (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{e}. \end{aligned} \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{2SLS}} - \boldsymbol{\beta} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{e}. } \]

Compare this with the ordinary least-squares estimation error:

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{OLS}} - \boldsymbol{\beta} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{e}. \]

The mathematical structures are closely related.

In both cases, the estimation error is obtained by multiplying the regression errors by a matrix determined by the observed explanatory variables and, for 2SLS, the instruments.

However, the matrices multiplying the error vector differ.

In particular, the validity of instrumental-variable estimation depends on the relationship between \(\mathbf{W}\) and \(\mathbf{e}\), rather than requiring the original explanatory variables to satisfy the corresponding orthogonality condition.

As with the single-instrument estimator, instrument exogeneity does not generally guarantee finite-sample unbiasedness of 2SLS.

We can, however, establish consistency under suitable conditions.

9.2.8.1 Consistency of Two-Stage Least Squares

To study the behavior of 2SLS as the sample size increases, write

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{2SLS}} - \boldsymbol{\beta} = \left( \frac{\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{X}}{n} \right)^{-1} \left( \frac{\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{e}}{n} \right). \]

Using the definition of \(\mathbf{P}_W\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{X}}{n} &= \frac{\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{W}}{n} \left( \frac{\mathbf{W}'\mathbf{W}}{n} \right)^{-1} \frac{\mathbf{W}'\mathbf{X}}{n}, \end{aligned} \]

and similarly,

\[ \frac{\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{e}}{n} = \frac{\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{W}}{n} \left( \frac{\mathbf{W}'\mathbf{W}}{n} \right)^{-1} \frac{\mathbf{W}'\mathbf{e}}{n}. \]

Suppose that the observations are independent and identically distributed, with suitable finite moments, and define

\[ \mathbf{Q}_{WW} = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}_i\mathbf{w}_i'], \]

and

\[ \mathbf{Q}_{WX} = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}_i\mathbf{x}_i']. \]

Assume that \(\mathbf{Q}_{WW}\) is positive definite and that \(\mathbf{Q}_{WX}\) has full column rank.

By the law of large numbers,

\[ \frac{\mathbf{W}'\mathbf{W}}{n} \xrightarrow{p} \mathbf{Q}_{WW}, \]

and

\[ \frac{\mathbf{W}'\mathbf{X}}{n} \xrightarrow{p} \mathbf{Q}_{WX}. \]

If the instruments satisfy

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}_i e_i] = \mathbf{0}, \]

then

\[ \frac{\mathbf{W}'\mathbf{e}}{n} \xrightarrow{p} \mathbf{0}. \]

Consequently,

\[ \frac{\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{e}}{n} \xrightarrow{p} \mathbf{0}, \]

while

\[ \frac{\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{P}_W\mathbf{X}}{n} \xrightarrow{p} \mathbf{Q}_{XW} \mathbf{Q}_{WW}^{-1} \mathbf{Q}_{WX}, \]

where \(\mathbf{Q}_{XW}=\mathbf{Q}_{WX}'\).

The limiting matrix is positive definite under the stated rank conditions. Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{2SLS}} \xrightarrow{p} \boldsymbol{\beta}. } \]

This derivation establishes the consistency of 2SLS under the stated sampling, exogeneity, and rank conditions.

Importantly, the argument does not require the zero conditional mean assumption

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}] = \mathbf{0}. \]

Instead, it requires the alternative population moment condition

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}_i e_i] = \mathbf{0}, \]

together with enough information in the instruments to identify the coefficients.

This result also explains why instrument relevance matters beyond ensuring that a finite-sample matrix can be inverted.

If the relationship between the instruments and explanatory variables is too weak, the estimator can behave poorly even when the instruments are exogenous. In particular, weak instruments can produce substantial finite-sample bias and highly variable estimates, making conventional large-sample approximations unreliable.

9.2.9 Interpretation

Instrumental-variable estimation illustrates an important consequence of weakening the classical mean assumption.

The failure of

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}\mid\mathbf{X}] = \mathbf{0} \]

does not necessarily imply that the parameter vector \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) cannot be estimated.

Instead, it means that the conditional relationship between the observed explanatory variables and the response may be insufficient to recover the coefficients of the specified data-generating mechanism through ordinary least squares.

Additional observed variables may provide alternative identifying information.

Under suitable instrument exogeneity and relevance conditions, this information can be used to construct estimators that are consistent for \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\).

However, the additional assumptions must be justified by the data-generating mechanism. Merely finding a variable correlated with an endogenous explanatory variable is not sufficient to establish that it is a valid instrument.

Furthermore, recovering the coefficients of the specified linear data-generating mechanism does not automatically assign those coefficients a causal interpretation.

The distinction between ordinary least squares and instrumental-variable estimation is therefore not primarily one of computational complexity.

Both methods use familiar matrix operations, and two-stage least squares is built directly from the projection geometry introduced earlier.

The essential difference is the statistical information used to identify the parameters.

Ordinary least squares relies on relationships involving the explanatory variables themselves.

Instrumental-variable estimation replaces these with relationships involving additional variables that satisfy alternative orthogonality conditions.

This provides an example of a more general principle:

The statistical properties of an estimator depend not only on its algebraic construction, but also on the parameter being estimated and the probabilistic assumptions that justify the information used for estimation.

Thus, a violation of the mean assumption may invalidate the usual justification for ordinary least squares without ruling out consistent estimation through other identifying conditions.
