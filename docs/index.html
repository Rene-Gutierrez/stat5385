1.2 Course Overview

This course presents Linear Regression through three complementary perspectives. The material begins with regression as a prediction and optimization problem, develops it into a statistical model through probability, and concludes by showing how several classical statistical methods arise as direct applications of the linear regression framework.

The course is therefore organized into three parts:

  1. Linear Regression for Prediction — a machine learning perspective based primarily on linear algebra and optimization, without probability.
  2. Linear Regression as a Statistical Model — probability is introduced to study uncertainty, sampling behavior, estimation, and inference.
  3. Applications of Linear Regression — methods such as ANOVA, experimental design, and instrumental variables are developed as consequences or extensions of the regression theory established in the first two parts.

Throughout the course, theoretical results are complemented with examples and R code. Computation is used to illustrate algebraic results, investigate statistical properties, and connect the mathematical theory with data analysis.

The objective is not to treat prediction, statistical inference, and classical statistical methods as separate subjects. Instead, the course develops a common mathematical framework from which each of them can be understood.

1.3 Part I — Linear Regression for Prediction

The first part approaches linear regression from a machine learning perspective. The objective is prediction: given explanatory variables, construct a function that produces useful predictions of a response.

At this stage, no probabilistic model is required. The observed data are treated as fixed quantities, and regression is formulated as an optimization problem. The principal mathematical tools are linear algebra, geometry, and optimization.

The development begins with the mathematical tools needed for regression and a conceptual introduction to the regression problem. It then progresses through increasingly general models.

1.3.1 Mathematical Prerequisites

The course begins with the mathematical tools required for the deterministic treatment of regression, with particular emphasis on linear algebra.

Concepts such as linear independence, column spaces, rank, matrix inverses, positive definite matrices, projections, and matrix decompositions provide the language needed to understand least squares geometrically and algebraically.

The purpose of this material is not to provide a general review of mathematics, but to introduce the tools that will be used directly in subsequent derivations.

1.3.2 Introduction to Linear Regression

Linear regression is introduced as a method for using explanatory variables to predict a response.

The emphasis is initially on the structure of the prediction problem: identifying the response, selecting explanatory variables, choosing a model, estimating its parameters, evaluating predictions, and examining discrepancies between observed and predicted values.

This establishes the modeling perspective that motivates the mathematical developments that follow.

1.3.3 Simple Linear Regression

Simple linear regression introduces least squares using a single explanatory variable.

The least squares estimator is derived as the solution to an optimization problem, and its properties are studied using algebra and geometry rather than probability.

This setting introduces many of the central ideas of the course, including fitted values, residuals, orthogonality, centering and standardization, the coefficient of determination, and residual analysis.

1.3.4 Polynomial Regression

Polynomial regression extends the prediction framework to nonlinear relationships while preserving linearity in the unknown coefficients.

It provides a natural bridge between simple and multiple regression: transformations of a single explanatory variable generate multiple predictors and demonstrate that apparently nonlinear prediction problems can still be treated using the linear regression machinery.

1.3.5 Multiple Linear Regression

Multiple linear regression develops the general matrix formulation of least squares.

The model is expressed in terms of a design matrix, and estimation is studied through the geometry of projections onto its column space. The normal equations, hat matrix, residual projection, sums of squares, \(R^2\), centering, standardization, and the relationships among regression coefficients are developed from this perspective.

By the end of Part I, students can view linear regression as a complete prediction algorithm. Given \(\mathbf{X}\) and \(\mathbf{y}\), they can derive the least squares solution, understand its geometry, evaluate model fit, and generate predictions without requiring any probabilistic assumptions.

1.4 Part II — Linear Regression as a Statistical Model

The second part introduces probability.

The central question changes. In Part I, the problem was:

Given the observed data, what linear predictor should we use?

In Part II, the question becomes:

What can we say about the behavior and uncertainty of that predictor if the observed data are generated by a stochastic process?

The quantities developed in Part I remain unchanged. The least squares estimator, fitted values, residuals, projection matrices, and sums of squares are not redefined. Instead, probability provides an additional layer that allows us to study their expected values, variances, distributions, and inferential properties.

1.4.1 Bootstrapping and the Introduction of Uncertainty

Bootstrapping provides an initial computational approach to uncertainty.

By repeatedly resampling the observed data, students can examine how estimated regression coefficients and predictions vary across samples. This provides a concrete introduction to sampling variability before deriving the corresponding results analytically from a probabilistic model.

Bootstrap confidence and prediction intervals also establish the distinction between uncertainty about an estimated regression function and uncertainty about a future observation.

1.4.2 Mean and Variance Assumptions

The regression model is then supplemented with assumptions about the conditional mean and variance of the errors.

These assumptions allow the expectation and variance of the response, coefficient estimates, fitted values, and residuals to be derived directly from the matrix results established in Part I.

This leads naturally to properties such as unbiasedness and to the Gauss–Markov theorem, which characterizes ordinary least squares among linear unbiased estimators.

The emphasis is on identifying exactly which statistical conclusions follow from each assumption.

1.4.3 The Normality Assumption and Statistical Inference

Normality provides the additional distributional structure required for classical finite-sample inference.

The sampling distributions of the least squares estimators and the variance estimator are derived, leading to confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, prediction intervals, partial and overall \(F\)-tests, and power calculations.

The statistical theory therefore emerges progressively:

\[ \text{Least Squares} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{Mean and Variance Assumptions} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{Normality} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{Inference}. \]

By the end of Part II, regression is no longer only a prediction method. It is a statistical model in which the uncertainty associated with estimation, prediction, and scientific conclusions can be quantified.

1.5 Part III — Applications of the Linear Regression Framework

The final part of the course develops important statistical methodologies as applications of the theory established in Parts I and II.

Rather than introducing these methods as independent collections of formulas, the objective is to show that they can be understood using the same ideas already developed for linear regression: design matrices, projections, least squares, covariance, hypothesis testing, and experimental structure.

1.5.1 Analysis of Variance and Analysis of Covariance

ANOVA is presented as a linear regression model in which categorical variables are represented through indicator variables.

Differences between group means become regression coefficients, and classical ANOVA sums of squares become decompositions associated with projections onto nested linear spaces.

ANCOVA follows naturally by including additional explanatory variables. Partial \(F\)-tests, adjustment for covariates, and the effect of randomization can therefore be understood using the same regression framework developed earlier in the course.

1.5.2 Experimental Design

Experimental design is studied through its effect on the design matrix and, consequently, on the information available for estimating regression coefficients.

Randomization, balance, replication, blocking, and the inclusion of control variables can be interpreted through their consequences for bias, variance, orthogonality, and power.

This perspective connects the mathematical properties of \(\mathbf{X}\) directly to decisions about how data should be collected.

Rather than treating experimental design as separate from regression, the course emphasizes that the design of an experiment determines the structure of the regression problem that will ultimately be analyzed.

1.5.3 Instrumental Variables

Instrumental variables illustrate what happens when the assumptions required for ordinary least squares are not satisfied, particularly when an explanatory variable is related to the error term.

The method builds on concepts already developed throughout the course, including projections, fitted values, orthogonality, and the interpretation of regression coefficients.

Instrumental-variable estimation can therefore be understood as an extension of the regression framework rather than as an unrelated estimation procedure.

1.5.4 A Unified View of Applications

The applications in this part demonstrate that many methods traditionally presented as separate statistical procedures can instead be viewed through a common framework.

ANOVA becomes regression with categorical predictors. ANCOVA becomes regression with group indicators and controls. Experimental design determines the structure and information content of the design matrix. Instrumental variables modify the regression procedure when ordinary least squares cannot identify the desired effect.

The purpose of Part III is therefore not primarily to introduce additional formulas. It is to demonstrate the generality of the regression framework developed in the first two parts.

1.6 Summary

The course follows a deliberate progression:

  1. Prediction without probability Linear regression is developed as an optimization problem using linear algebra. Least squares, projections, residuals, polynomial regression, and multiple regression are studied from a machine learning perspective.

  2. Probability and statistical inference Probability is added to the existing regression framework. Sampling variability, expectations, variances, distributions, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, prediction intervals, and power are derived from increasingly strong assumptions.

  3. Applications derived from regression Classical statistical methods are developed as consequences and extensions of the regression framework, including ANOVA, ANCOVA, experimental design, and instrumental variables.

This organization separates three ideas that are often introduced simultaneously:

\[ \boxed{\text{Prediction}} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\text{Uncertainty and Inference}} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\text{Statistical Applications}}. \]

Students first learn how linear regression works, then why its statistical conclusions are valid, and finally how the same theory generates a broader collection of statistical methods.

Throughout the course, mathematical derivations and R implementations are developed together. The objective is for students to understand linear regression not simply as a collection of formulas or software commands, but as a unified mathematical framework for prediction, statistical inference, and the analysis of research data.
